Chapter 1 · Where our wood comes from

Timber with a history.

Much of our teak comes from old joglos, traditional Javanese houses with tall roofs supported by four main pillars and timber joinery.

As these buildings are dismantled, we recover timber for another use.

SATU uses reclaimed teak (Tectona grandis) from houses, bridges, boats and other structures. The live Another Life Passport connects the furniture with its recorded origins.

Old posts and beams, marked and sorted before they are cut.

Why origin matters

Look beyond the word reclaimed.

Documented origins help distinguish reclaimed timber from newly harvested wood. The research below explains why sourcing matters.

What the research shows · Logging and land use

  1. 01

    Gaveau and colleagues mapped approximately 272,000 km of primary logging roads built on Borneo between 1973 and 2010.

  2. 02

    Their research highlights the importance of protecting logged forests from fire and conversion to plantations.

  3. 03

    By 2010, industrial oil-palm and timber plantations covered about 10% of Borneo. These findings describe regional land-use change, not the origin of any individual teak batch.

And teak?

Age affects teak properties.

A 2018 study compared 10-year-old, short-rotation teak with 40-year-old, long-rotation teak:

The younger wood was also less resistant to decay in the study. These results describe the tested timber, not measured performance of SATU. Reclaimed structural teak lets us work with older timber that is already available.

Research on reuse

23–92%

Lower climate impact across reuse scenarios in a study of a wooden house.

A 2024 study in Buildings reported 23–92% lower global warming potential than its reference scenario, depending on the extent of reuse. This is building research, not a carbon assessment of our furniture.

Chapter 2 · In development

Meet ulin, Borneo's ironwood.

Ulin (Eusideroxylon zwageri), also known as Borneo ironwood, is a dense, slow-growing timber traditionally used for boats, bridges and waterside structures.

Our first reclaimed-ulin samples have been made. It remains in development as our second material.

Our sourcing approach

We work only with reclaimed ulin, with its origins traced and documented. We do not source newly felled ulin.

Chapter 3 · Shou Sugi Ban, our way

Fire, borrowed from Japan.

Yakisugi, often called Shou Sugi Ban, is a Japanese technique traditionally used to char cedar.

We adapt the process for reclaimed teak, darkening the surface with flame and finishing it with natural oil. It brings out the grain and gives SATU its deep colour.

Chapter 4 · Natural oil

Into the wood, not on top of it.

The finish is part of ongoing care.

Natural oil penetrates the wood. The surface can be re-oiled when it needs care.

Marks from the timber’s first life remain visible wherever they do not compromise strength.

Making

Made in Indonesia.

Every piece is made by hand in Indonesia by makers we work with directly, in small batches.

Producing close to the material keeps the supply chain short and visible.

Stacks of repurposed teak, drilled one board at a time.

The hardware

Joints made to open again.

Repair begins with a joint that can be dismantled.

Stainless steel (SUS) bolts screw into threaded inserts set in the wood. The bolts engage with the inserts rather than cutting into the timber, allowing repeated disassembly.

Three-bolt clusters secure the main joints and give SATU its distinctive detail.

The sign in the bolts

Three dots. One circle.

The three bolts also echo the idea behind Another Life:

Material

The wood already lived once, before it reached our workshop.

Owner

The piece can move on to someone new and keep working.

Place

Undo the bolts and it can be rebuilt somewhere else.

Built to be rebuilt

Flat, bolted, and back again.

Every piece ships flat and bolts together on site.

It can be dismantled for moving, storage or individual component replacement.

Shipping

More chairs in each container.

Flat-packing reduces the space needed for transport.

We estimate a 40 ft high-cube container holds around 1,300 flat-packed chairs, compared with roughly 350 assembled chairs: almost four times as many per load.

Another Life estimates; final capacity depends on packaging and loading. This comparison is not a measured emissions saving.
