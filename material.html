Chapter 1 · Where our wood comes from

17,000 islands, built in wood.

Indonesia has more than 17,000 islands, and for centuries much of it was built in wood.

Today many of them are making way for concrete. The teak inside is often older than our grandparents. Throw it away? Not on our watch. We'd rather give it another life.

Our teak is repurposed: timber that has already lived a life, often in old joglo houses, and also in bridges, boats and other structures. We are also building a way to trace each piece's own story: the Passport.

Old posts and beams, marked and sorted before they are cut.

Reclaimed? Not always.

Ask where it stood.

“Reclaimed” is one of the easiest words to put on a label, and one of the hardest to check. It matters, because a new tree is never just one tree.

And teak?

New teak isn’t old teak.

Most new teak in Indonesia is grown in plantations, and some of it is cut very young. A 2018 study compared 10-year-old plantation teak with 40-year-old teak:

The young wood was also less resistant to rot. The teak in an old joglo grew slowly, often for many decades, before it was ever cut. That kind of teak is no longer easy to buy new. Reclaiming it is how we work with it.

Chapter 2 · Shou Sugi Ban, our way

Fire, borrowed from Japan.

In Japan, carpenters have protected wood by setting it on fire for centuries. Really. It's called yakisugi, and a charred surface helps wood stand up to weather and bugs.

We borrowed the idea and tried it on something harder: repurposed teak. We burn the surface, then rub in natural oil. The idea is to help the wood handle the sun and almost any weather.

An old Japanese technique, moved to a hard wood.

Every piece is made by hand in Indonesia by makers we work with directly, in small batches.

Producing close to the material keeps the supply chain short and visible.

The hardware

Bolts built to outlive the chair.

Hardware is usually the last thing anyone thinks about. It is often the cheapest part of a piece of furniture, and the first to fail: screws rust, threads strip, and a good chair is thrown out because of a part that cost almost nothing.

We turned that around. Every SATU joint is held by engineered stainless steel (SUS) bolts that screw into threaded inserts set into the wood. The bolt can be opened and closed again and again without wearing out.

The three-bolt clusters you see on every piece aren't decoration. They hold everything together, and they're designed to last longer than the furniture around them.

The sign in the bolts

Three dots. One circle.

Look closely at any SATU joint and you'll find three bolts. They hold the piece together, and they also carry the idea behind the name. Each bolt stands for a life the piece can have, and together they form the letter A, for ANOTHER — LIFE.

Shipping

Stop shipping air.

An assembled chair is mostly empty space: between the legs, under the seat, behind the backrest. Ship it like that and you pay to move air across the ocean.

Flat-packed, a 40 ft high-cube container holds around 1,300 of our chairs, against roughly 350 assembled. Almost four times as many chairs per trip, so fewer containers, less fuel and a lower cost for every chair.
